    1. Flux Out of a Box.

    Solution.

    The box is closed, so the divergence theorem turns six surface integrals into one triple integral. Here
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \pi\cos(\pi x) + 3y^2z + 2z . \end{equation*}
    The first term integrates to \(\left[\sin(\pi x)\right]_{x_0}^{x_1} = 0\text{,}\) because both endpoints are integers and \(\sin\) vanishes at every integer multiple of \(\pi\text{.}\) That is why it contributes nothing at all.
    🔗
    For the rest, with \(w = x_1 - x_0\) and \(\int_0^1 3y^2\, dy = 1\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = w\int_{z_0}^{z_1}\!\!\int_0^1 \left(3y^2z + 2z\right) dy\, dz = w\int_{z_0}^{z_1} 3z\, dz = \frac{3w}{2}\left(z_1^2 - z_0^2\right). \end{equation*}

    5. One Field, Three Theorems.

    Solution.

    (a) On the curve \(C\) we have \(z \equiv 0\text{,}\) so the third component of \(\mathbf{F}\) contributes \(z^2\, dz = 0\) along \(C\text{.}\) The line integral is therefore genuinely planar:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \oint_C -y^3\, dx + x^3\, dy. \end{equation*}
    By Green’s theorem, (3.1.6), with \(M = -y^3\) and \(N = x^3\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C -y^3\, dx + x^3\, dy = \iint_D \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) dA = \iint_D 3\left(x^2 + y^2\right) dA. \end{equation*}
    In polar coordinates,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_D 3\left(x^2 + y^2\right) dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1 3r^2 \cdot r\, dr\, d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \frac{3}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{2} \approx 4.712. \end{equation*}
    (b) The curl is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} \amp \mathbf{j} \amp \mathbf{k} \\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ -y^3 \amp x^3 \amp z^2 \end{vmatrix} = \left(3x^2 + 3y^2\right) \mathbf{k}. \end{equation*}
    Stokes’ theorem asserts \(\displaystyle\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma\text{.}\) Since \(C\) also bounds the flat disk \(D\) (with upward normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{k}\)), we may evaluate the flux there:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_D \left(3x^2 + 3y^2\right) \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{k}\, dA = \iint_D 3\left(x^2 + y^2\right) dA = \frac{3\pi}{2}, \end{equation*}
    in agreement with (a). As for surface independence: Stokes’ theorem equates the flux of \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\) through any oriented surface with boundary \(C\) to the single number \(\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\text{.}\) The boundary circulation pins down the answer; the interior of the surface is irrelevant—this is the point of Only the Boundary Matters. Hence every surface spanning \(C\) carries curl-flux \(\frac{3\pi}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) The divergence is \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(-y^3\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(x^3\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(z^2\right) = 2z\text{.}\) By the divergence theorem, Theorem 3.3.5, the outward flux through the closed surface \(S \cup D\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_{S \cup D} \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \iiint_E 2z\, dV. \end{equation*}
    Using cylindrical coordinates with \(0 \le z \le 1 - r^2\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_E 2z\, dV = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-r^2} 2z\, r\, dz\, dr\, d\theta = 2\pi \int_0^1 \left(1 - r^2\right)^2 r\, dr = 2\pi \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{\pi}{3} \approx 1.047. \end{equation*}
    On the bottom disk \(D\) the outward normal is \(-\mathbf{k}\) and \(\mathbf{F} \cdot (-\mathbf{k}) = -z^2 = 0\) since \(z = 0\) there. The bottom contributes nothing, so the paraboloid alone carries the full flux:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \frac{\pi}{3}. \end{equation*}
    (d) Suppose \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) are two surfaces sharing the boundary \(C\text{,}\) oriented consistently. Reversing the orientation of \(S_2\) and gluing gives a closed surface enclosing a solid region \(W\text{.}\) For the curl field, the divergence theorem gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_{S_1} (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma - \iint_{S_2} (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \iiint_W \nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F})\, dV = 0, \end{equation*}
    because \(\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0\) identically. Surface independence in (b) is thus equivalent to the vanishing divergence of a curl. The field \(\mathbf{F}\) itself enjoys no such privilege: \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 2z \not\equiv 0\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma - \iint_D \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \iiint_E 2z\, dV = \frac{\pi}{3} \ne 0, \end{equation*}
    and indeed we found \(\frac{\pi}{3}\) through the paraboloid versus \(0\) through the disk. The moral: circulation-type quantities (curl-flux) depend only on the boundary curve, while flux-type quantities depend on the surface itself—precisely because curls are divergence-free and general fields are not.
